Those who know me know that I am a big fan of “**zero-touch”**: collecting, structuring, organising and reconciling information without any human hands being involved. As part of my recent AI training, I decided to rebuild my former champagne website, which I maintained myself back in 1999 with blood, sweat and tears, but this time using modern AI techniques and in such a way that the content of the new site is discovered fully automatically and updated daily. And yes, it was a bit hard to swallow; I have said goodbye to PHP, DotNetNuke, WordPress and other faded glories. I am curious to see when AI will make JavaScript redundant too.

## THE PLAN:

I wanted to enable visitors to a revamped website [**https://www.Champagneinfo.nl**](https://www.Champagneinfo.nl) to search information on all the champagnes from all the producers in the 300+ French champagne villages. I also thought it would be a fun idea to have AI figure out which importer(s) import which champagnes from which producers. The champagne world is just as complex as anywhere else, so there are firms that operate under multiple trade names and also maintain multiple websites, and champagne houses get taken over and/or merged. Ergo: an administrative tangle of information. Fortunately, there are official bodies that you can now consult about champagne in an automated way. It turns out that you can model the entire champagne world in about 30 interlinked tables. **But then, how do you keep their content complete, accurate and comprehensive?**

In the past, I spent whole evenings researching on the internet, bought all sorts of champagne books (you know: those things with paper pages in them) and updated my website based on what I had found or read. We already had IT at the start of this century, so I had an MS-SQL database that I kept up to date by hand. Based on that database, I generated various web pages with content, including for www.NL-Champagne.com (R.I.P.), [**www.lesabredor.nl**](https://www.lesabredor.nl) and [**Champagne.startpagina.nl**](https://Champagne.startpagina.nl). Technically, that worked rather well, if I say so myself, but -as is often the case when you maintain data by hand- I suffered a lot from duplicates and creative spelling, and the sites were rarely in line with reality. Back then that wasn't such a big deal; the bar wasn't set very high at the time.

The "**Nederlandse Champagne Paginas**" (Dutch Champagne Pages) that I maintained at the time contained 100 A4 pages of Dutch-language information about ‘le' and ‘la' champagne. As a variation on the coffee cam in Cambridge, I had a champagne cam. Within the site, I had created an international corner with all kinds of useful lists of (websites of) hundreds of champagne villages, crus, producers, champagnes and importers. At the time, it was not feasible to properly keep track of the relationships between the records in those tables. I had just about managed to group the producers by region and village, but that was more or less where it ended. After the site had reached 1 million page views (which was quite a lot back then), I hung up the champagne websites and my champagne import business.

## A COMPLETE AI WEBSITE WITHIN 24 HOURS

I started again last night, with nothing but some prehistoric knowledge of which organisations are involved with champagne. For instance, there are still French folks who have neatly classified the 300+ champagne villages by region and soil quality. Another French organisation has put 500 champagne producers/cellars (those that were willing to pay for it) on a website. The French government has handed out numbers here and there and has neatly published those on a website too.

Other people who build champagne websites mostly limit themselves to the low-hanging fruit and find/show little more than those 300+ villages and 500 large/expensive champagne houses. I remembered from the beginning of this century that there are a few thousand producers active in France, including a whole load of small houses that don't want to pay a lot of money to be registered everywhere. Since I want to host the largest and most complete champagne information on my site, I had to look for other sources. The answer turned out to be simple: every firm that takes itself seriously has a website describing what it can do/supply. It makes no difference whether it is a champagne producer, importer or shop.

  

The first challenge was to have it find out which websites mention anything about champagne. Then you need to classify the firms' sites you have found into types of sites and -depending on the type of site- look for useful information that you can then summarise on a producer, village or importer card. Then you still need to link the cards (per town, producer, importer, champagne, website, source, etc.) together, and you're done. In the past, doing that with PHP scripts and web crawlers would have been a nightmare, but nowadays we have AI, and it turns out to be very good at searching, research and summarising.

  

You can already ask AI for a lot of information directly (the thing has already fully mapped the internet). You can also ask AI to summarise the content of a website. These days you can happily ask: “Which champagnes are made by this producer?” or “From which producers does this firm import its champagne(s)?”. Collecting the information once is one thing. Having it maintained automatically and affordably is a whole different story. I took three approaches: I defined a few “refresh” buttons that, when clicked, re-mapped the producer's site and, if that information was available, linked the champagnes and importers. I defined a “rummager” that neatly classifies all websites containing the word “champagne” and marks them as useful or irrelevant. Third, I created an “analyst” that summarises and analyses site content and puts it on the relevant cards on my website, even when I'm not at my PC myself; it costs some AI tokens, but you get something for it.

  

Once you have collected the data, it is -even without AI- not really complicated to display that data on a site. It is, of course, even more fun to have AI translate the content in real time and to generate the page content with the help of AI as well. It got really fun when the content was made accessible in a chat-like way. You can now ask the champagne website, for example: “Give me the champagnes made from 100% Pinot Noir that are imported by Amsterdam-based firms”

I only told ChatGPT that I wanted to make the site available with a Dutch, English and French user interface. I “talk” to my site in Dutch, the site chats with French websites as if it were nothing, and the result is neatly presented in four languages. If I had wanted to, I could also have added Chinese as a working language, even though I don't speak fluent Chinese myself.

  

I also had a few things added under the hood so that I can keep track of what is happening on the site. For instance, I have a page that shows exactly, per day, which cards have been added, deleted and/or modified. I can browse through the 24 tables and export and back up the content. Also not unimportant, as it turns out: I have a page where I can see per day how many AI tokens the site consumes and what that costs me. There are also nice statistics under the hood of ChatGPT, so that without any tinkering I can easily keep track of what is happening on the site and what does or doesn't work well.

For anyone who also wants to build a nice website with the help of OpenAI, you will need:

-   A paid OPENAI/CHATGPT account (CHATGPT Plus is sufficient)
-   Various OpenAI plugins (SITES, DEVELOPER): you will automatically be asked to approve something.
-   Optional: AI tokens if you want to retrieve information from others via your website,
-   An internet domain name from, for example, TransIP (OPENAI provides all other hosting facilities)

What I found most astonishing about the AI experiment was that you didn't have to do any programming for it. Just by chatting with my AI partner (ChatGPT), together we put together (within 24 hours!!!) a rather nice site, and the site automatically updates its content using content that others publish on their own websites and maintain there themselves - in their own language.
